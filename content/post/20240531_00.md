## Metric Space
Picture this, it's a sunny Sunday afternoon, and we're sitting on a bench in the park.
We've met to have a detailed conversation about the arena fight in Dune 2 - or 
more specifically - about the __distances__ between ```points```in a ```Set```of points.

A __metric space__ is a set $X$, together with a __distance function__ $d$.
The distance function will assign a real number (a decimal number) to each pair of points in the set, $X$.
This number represents the __distance__ between the points.

In other words, if we imagine the Arena populated by ten gladiators, then our distance
function will give us the distance between every pair of gladiators in the arena.

> Metric Spaces are essential because they formalize the notion of _distance_
and give us tools to discuss complicated topics related to distance.

Before we proceed in our discussion, let's take the time to write down the definition of a metric space so that we agree and can move forward:

### Requirements of our distance function
1.  __Non-negativity:__ The distance between a pair of gladiators is never less than zero (this requirement is necessary otherwise two gladiators could occupy the exact same physical space in the arena, which makes no sense.). We formalize this requirement as: $d(x,y) \geq 0$. 
2.  __Identity:__ The distance between a gladiator and itself is always 0, which we formalize as $d(x,y)=0 \text{ if and only if } d=y$ (which translates into: _if the distance between gladiator $x$ and gladiator $y$ is zero, then they are in fact the same gladiator_).
3.  __Symmetry:__ The distance between gladiator A, Feyd-Rautha, and gladiator B, , is the same as the distance between B and A: $d(x,y) = d(y,x)$.
4. __Triangle inequality:__ In an arena with only 2 gladiotors and 1 lion, the distance
between the gladiators x and y, cannot exceed the combined distance between gladiator A and B, and gladiator B and the lion, $d(x,z) \leq d(x,y) + d(y,z)$. 


### Requirement: Lipschitzian Maps for contraction
This post is about _contraction_, so let's take a moment and ask ourself, 
what does _that_ even mean?

A function between two metric spaces, $f:(X,d_X) \rightarrow (Y,d_Y)$ is said to be Lipschitz continuous if there exists a non-negative __constant__ $K \geq 0$ such that for each pair of points $x_1, x_2 \in X$ we can multiply the distance between the pair $(x_1,x_2)$ in the arena $X$ with the constant $K$ and we will get a contraction of the arena with is equal in size or smaller.
Formally we can write this as:
$$K \cdot d_X(x_1,x_2) \geq d_Y(f(x_1),f(x_2))$$

The smallest such constant $K$, is called the _Lipschitz constant of $f$.

> Uniformly continous is a stronger form of continuity.

> Bounded variation: Lipschitz continous functions do not oscillate wildly. Their rate of change is bounded.



We want to highlight a particular type of function that we'll use.  
A function that we can apply to every point in our set, and every result
from using the function will itself be a point in the arean.
In other words, the function maps a set onto itself:
$F: X \rightarrow X$. Now, this function is said to be _Lipschitzian_ if
there exists a _constant_ $\alpha \geq 0$ such that:

$$d(F(x), F(y)) \leq \alpha \cdot d(x,y) \text{ for all } x,y \in X \tag{1}\label{eq1}$$

Equation $\eqref{eq1}$ is called the _Global Stiffness Equestion_ and is the
focus of this post.

#### Give me the smallest contraction
The smallest $\alpha$ for which $\eqref{eq1}$ holds is called 
_the Lipschitz constant_ for $F$, and is given its own denotation, $L$.

If $L < 1$ then we say that $F$ is a __contraction__, basically what this means
is that if we apply function $F$ to every pair $(x,y)$ in the Harkonnen Arena (the set $X$)
then the entire arena will _contract_ in size (think, we scale it down).

If, on the other hand, $L=1$ then we call $F$ a __nonexpansive__, meaning, we
can apply the function to every pair of points$(x,y)$ in the arena, without
worrying that the size of the arena will _increase_.

### A note on uniqueness and existence
You sit in a sofa. On a table in front of you, there's a bowl of a 100 M&M's. 
Just like Lady Gaga, you only like the red ones.
Problem is, when you look at the top surface of the M&Ms, you see every color of
M&M _except_ red ones.
You're about the scrunge around in the bowl, and look for red M&Ms, but two
thoughts strike you just as you're about to start scounging around:
1. __Existence:__ Are there even _any_ red M&M's in this bowl? Perhaps someone removed them
and I'll be wasting my precious time and energy. That's a question of __existence__.
2. __Uniqueness:__ The second question you have is, well let's assume there's at least one red M&M in this bowl, is it the only one - or are there more than one?
If I find a red M&M, should I keep looking for another, and for how long?
That's a question of __uniqueness__.

Both these qualities are releveant to our discussion - let's explore how.

## Theorem 1.1 The Banach Contraction Principle
Let $(X,d)$ be a complete metric space and let $F : X \rightarrow X$ be a contraction
with Lipschitzian constant $L$. Then $F$ has a __unique fixed point__ $u \in X$.
Furhtermore, for any $x \in X$ we have that
$$\lim_{n \to \infty}F^n(x) = u$$

Picture this,
you sit down with a cup of coffee, pour some sugar in, and stir with a silver spoon.
Now the question is, will __at least one__ sugar particle remain in its position until the stirring has stopped?

Fixed Point Theory says it will.


According to Brouwer's fixed-point theorem, it is true that _at least one_ sugar particle will return to the exact same position it had before we stirred the coffee.

Brouwer's theorem applies to __continuous functions mapping__ from a compact convex set to itself, and it guarantees the existence of at least one fixed point.

If we consider the surface of the coffee as a two-dimensional disk (a compact convex set), and the stirring motion as a continuous function that moves points on this disk, Brouwer's fixed-point theorem states that there is at least one point on the surface of the coffee that remains in the same position before and after stirring.

Here's a more detailed explanation:

## Definition: Brouwer's Fixed-Point Theorem  
For any continuous function $f$ mapping a ```compact convex set``` _onto itself ```Euclidean Space```,   
there is __at least__ one point $x$, such that:
$$f(x)=x$$

## Model
1. __Compact Convex Set__: The surface of the coffee can be modeled as a two-dimensional disk, which is a __compact convex set__.
2. __Continuous Function__: The act of stirring the coffee can be seen as a continuous transformation of the points on the coffee surface.

Given these conditions, Brouwer's fixed-point theorem ensures that there exists at least one point on the coffee surface that remains in its original position despite the stirring.

Constraints:
Brouwer's theoretical result assumes ideal conditions such as:
1.  the surface is perfectly two-dimensional
2.  the stirring is assumed to be perfectly continuous without any disruptions
