## Diffusion Process - Three Directions
We diffuse the data by adding Gaussian noise to it in a step-wise manner.
There are three main ways to implement the diffusion model:
1. DDPM: denoising diffusion probabilistic model (the original)
2. NCSN: noise conditional score networks
3. SDE: stochastic differential equations

## Training
Let's start with something familiar, a __Gaussian Distribution__, and let's make things _concrete_ by coding a small Rust program to illustrate our abstractions.  
We know that a Gaussian (standard normal) distribution is characterized by its:
+ Bell-shaped curve
+ mean ( center of the distribution)
+ standard deviation, which controls the "width" of the bell curve


## Goal: Generative Battlefield Terrain Simulator
We want to train a simulator (program) (a Decoder) so that it is able to generate realistic outputs (like battlefield images), starting from random noise (Gaussian distriction). So given random input, learn how to generate something realistic.  
{{<indentline>}} To train our simulator we will provide it with _real battlefield images_ as examples.
Our simulator will assign high probability to these real examples that we provide it.
We optimize this by using _log-likelihood_ (a simplified product of probabilities).  
{{<indentline>}} Our ultimate goal is toe _minimize the difference_ between _real distributions_ and
_generated distributions_. This difference is called the ```KL divergence``.
Unfortunately, computing the KL divergence is computationally expensive and hard,
because we don't know all the hidden factors (called latent variables).

### Battlefield images
In our case, our aim is to genrate realistic battle scenes (for example drone surveillance images) from pure random noise.
Our training data consists of _real_ images from actual battlefield conditions (terrain, troops, tanks, etc).
Each image is represented as $x^1, x^2, \dots , x^m$, taken from a real-world distribution $p_{data}(x)$.  
Model - our program is represented by a single function $p_{\theta}(x)$, which is the _probability_ that our model assigns to generating a scene $x$.


---


## Step
Our goal is to create a ```Decoder``` which can _translate_ our gaussian distribution into _high-quality images_, for example of cats and dogs.
We need to find the _paramters_ $\theta$ of the ```Decoder```, $p_\theta(x)$ that can transform
a simple gaussian distribution into an _image distribution_

Since we do _not_ know what the _true_ _data distribution_ is, $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ and so we can only _approximate it_ by sample lot of images. If we let images of cats be represented by $x^1$ and so forth, we can...

$$\text{Dataset:}\\{x^1, x^2, \dots, x^m \\} \sim p_{\text{data}}(x) $$

---

We are trying to maximize how likely our simulator is to reproduce real battlefield images, on average.


## Training Goal / MLE
Large Language Models (LLM) have billions of parameters, but our model will have to be tiny in comparison.
In training our model we want to _adjust_ its parameters, which we denote $\theta$, so that the simulator is able to generate 
battlefield images that look like real-world battlefield images.  
{{<indentline>}}This is called the __Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)__ - in which we try to train the model to assign _high probability_ to actual images - think of these images as hills in a landscape.
is to _maximize_ the _sampling probability_, which is known as the _Maximum Likelihood_. Formally we can express this as
$$\theta^\star = \text{arg max} \prod^m_{i=1} p_{\theta} (x^i)$$

## A Look at MLE
We need to take a look at MLE and make sure we really understand it. Maximum Likelihood is a statistical method that we can use to _estimate_ the parameters of a model. The __likelihood function_, in our case $p_{\thete}(x)$, measures how _likely it is to ```observer``` the given data under different parameter settings.

Working with products of probabilities is numerically unstable.  
To mitigate, we take the log

By applying a _log function_ to our _MLE_, we get something that is easier to work with - turning the products $\prod$ into a sum $\sum$.

The expression still represent the _log-likelihood_ and our goal is still to increase the models's confidence in the real images.
Finally, if the samples $x^i \sim p_{data}(x)$ then the average log-probability can be rewritten as an expectation.
$$\begin{align} \theta^\star & = \text{argmax} \\; \text{log} \Biggl( \prod^m_{i=1} p_{\theta} (x^i) \Biggr) \\\
& = \text{arg max} \\; \sum^m_{i=1} \text{log} \\; p_{\theta} (x^i) \\\
& = \text{arg max}  \\; \mathbb{E_{x\sim p_{data}}} [\text{log} \\; p_{\theta} (x)]\end{align}$$

The definition is
$$\theta^\star = \text{arg max}  \\; \int p_{data}(x) \text{log} \\; p_{\theta} (x) dx$$

If we subtract a _constant_ that has nothing to do with the parameter $\theta$
then we find that this is just the _tail divergence_ between the distribution
from our generated model and the data distribution. 
We subtract a constant that does not depend on $\theta$.
Why? Because the second term is _fixed_ - it's the entropy of the real data and doesn't involve $\theta$.

> Clarify

$$\theta^\star = \text{arg max} \\; \int p_{data}(x) \text{log} \\; p_{\theta} (x) dx\\; - \int p_{data}(x) \text{log} \\; p_{\text{data}} (x) dx$$

We want our model distribution $p_{\theta}$ to get as close as possible to the real battlefield data distribution $p_{\text{data}}$. Meaning, we want to make sure that our battlefield 
image simulator behaves statistically _indinstiguishable_ from real-world imagse.

$$\begin{align}
\theta^\star & \approx \text{arg max} \\; \int p_{data}(x) \text{log} \\; \frac{p_{data}(x)}{p_{\theta} (x)}  dx\\; \\\ 
& = \text{arg min} \\; D_{KL}(p_{\text{data}} || p_{\theta})
\end{align}$$


So maximizing the likelihood means _minimizing_ the similarity between our two distributions
$p_{data}(x)$ and $p_{\theta}(x)$. 

## Computationally hard
To compute ```log p(x)``` exactly, we usually need to integrate over _latent variables_ ```z``` (underlying factors like mission objectives,, weather, troop morale, etc).
But this assumes that we know ```p(z | x)```, which we usually do not. That makes the computation hard (or even impossible).


It's hard to compute this likelihood value. Either we have to integrate out all latent variables z, $log \\;p(x) = log \int p(x,z) dz$,
or $log \\;p(x) = log \frac{p(x,z)}{p(z|x)}$, it assumes that we know the ground truth _latent encoder_ $p(z|x)$

---

## Work-around
So we're dealing with a computationally expensive problem, let's see what we can do to simplify the computation.

We start by introducing an ```Encoder``` that can capture the _latent variable probability_ given an observation $x$.
$$q(z | x) \leftarrow \text{Encoder} \leftarrow x$$
$$z \rightarrow \underset{\theta}{\text{Decoder}} \\; \rightarrow p_{\theta}(x | z)$$

We can express our $Encoder$ as

$$\text{log } p(x) = \text{log } p(x) \underbrace{\int q(z | x) \\;dz}_{\text{this term is one thing we are all latent variable z}}$$

$$\text{log } p(x) = \int q(z | x) \underbrace{\text{log } p(x)}_{\text{we move the log likelihood inside the integral}} dz$$

We (1) rewrite the expression as an _expectation_. Next we (2) apply the _Baye's Rules_, (3) multiply in a _dummy term_, (4) swap the posterior probability term, and finally (5) separate them.


$$\begin{align} \text{log } p(x) & =^{(1)} \mathbb{E_{q(z | x)}\Bigl[\text{log } p(x)} \Bigr] \\\
& =^{(2)} \mathbb{E_{q(z | x)}} \Bigl[ \text{log } \frac{\color{red}{p(x,z)}}{\color{blue}{p(z|x)}} \Bigr] \\\
& =^{(3)} \mathbb{E_{q(z | x)}} \Bigl[ \text{log } \frac{\color{red}{p(x,z)}\\;q(z|x)}{\color{blue}{p(z|x)}\\;q(z|x)} \Bigr] \\\
& =^{(4)} \mathbb{E_{q(z | x)}} \Bigl[ \text{log } \frac{\color{red}{p(x,z)}\\;q(z|x)}{q(z|x)\\;\color{blue}{p(z|x)}} \Bigr] \\\
& =^{(5)} \mathbb{E_{q(z | x)}} \Bigl[ \text{log } \frac{\color{red}{p(x,z)}}{q(z|x)} \Bigr] + \mathbb{E_{q(z | x)}} \Bigl[ \text{log } \frac{q(z|x)}{\color{blue}{p(z|x)}} \Bigr] \end{align}$$

We now recognize that the second term is a the _tail divergence_ between our ```Encoder```, given $q(z| x)$ and our ground truth encoder $\color{blue}{p(z|x)}$.
$$\text{log } p(x) = \mathbb{E_{q(z | x)}} \Bigl[ \text{log } \frac{\color{red}{p(x,z)}}{q(z|x)} \Bigr] + D_{KL}\bigl(q(z| x) \\;||\\; \color{blue}{p(z|x)}\bigr) \tag{1}\label{eq1}$$

Since we don't have access to the __ground truth__ encoder,$\color{blue}{p(z|x)}$ , this means that we don't know the value of $D_{KL }\bigl(q(z| x) \\;||\\; \color{blue}{p(z|x)}\bigr)$. However, we _do_ know that the __tail divergence__ is non-negative, $\ge 0$.
This means that the first term in expression $(1)$ is the __lower bound__ of the __log-likelihood value__ .

$$\text{log } p(x) \ge \mathbb{E_{q(z | x)}} \Bigl[ \text{log } \frac{\color{red}{p(x,z)}}{q(z|x)} \Bigr]  \tag{2}\label{eq2}$$


> The log-likelihood $(2)$ measures the statistical evidence for our model,  and is known as the __Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)__.
