#### 1a)
> Given two vectors $\mathbf{\vec{u}} = \[1,2,2\], \\; \mathbf{\vec{v}}=\[-1,2,5\]$, find a third vector $|\mathbf{\vec{w}}| \gt 0$, which is orthogonal to both vectors $\mathbf{\vec{u}}$ and $\mathbf{\vec{v}}$.

### Concept: The Dot Product
The __dot product__ is a way to multiply two vectors and get
a scalar value in return (a number, rather than another vector).
{{<indentline>}}The dot product can be geometrically interpreted as multiplying the lengths of Two vectors are orthogonal if their dotproduct is 0.

__Example__:  
$$\mathbf{a} = \[1,2,3\], \\; \mathbf{b}=\[4,5,6\], \\;  \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = (1\cdot4) + (2\cdot5) + (3\cdot6) := 4+10+18 = 32$$

We can write the dot product as, multiplying the magnitudes of two vectors by the angle $cos(\theta)$ between them:
$$\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|cos(\theta)$$

### Concept: The Cross Product
We can find a vector $\mathbf{c} \in R^3$ which is _orthogonal_ to _two other vectors_ $a$ and $b$, by 
calculating their __cross product__, which is a binary operation on two vectors in $R^3$. The result of the operation, denoted $a \times b$ is new vector that is _orthogonal_   (perpendicular) to the original vectors.

__Example__:  
$$\mathbf{a} = \[2,3,4\], \\; \mathbf{b}=\[5,6,7\],$$

$$
\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = 
    \begin{vmatrix}
    i & j & k \\\
    2 & 3 & 4 \\\
    5 & 6 & 7 \\\
    \end{vmatrix}
$$
where $i,j,k$ are the _standard basis vectors for the $x,y,z$ axes. The calculation is done as follows:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} &= \\\
&= (3 \cdot 7 - 4 \cdot 6)i - (2 \cdot 7 - 4 \cdot 5)j + (2 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 5)k \\\
&= (21-24)i - (14-20)j + (12-15)k \\\
&= -3i + 6j - 3k \\\
&= \[-3,6,-3\] \\\
\end{align}
$$

We can verify that our calculated vector $a \times b$ is indeed _orthogonal to $a$ and $b$, by using the _dot product_ (remember, for orthogonality the result should be $0$):

$$
\begin{align}
(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) \cdot \mathbf{a} &= \\\
&= \[-3,6,-3\] \cdot \[2,3,4\] \\\
&= (-3 \cdot 2) + (6 \cdot 3) + (-3 \cdot 4) \\\
&= -6 + 18 -12 \\\
&= 0 \\\
\end{align}
$$

We've shown that the vector $a$ is orthogonal to the cross product. Calculating $\(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}\) \cdot \mathbf{b}$ will show the same.

### Solution
We had two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, and we wanted to find a third vector $\mathbf{c}$, which was _orthogonal_ (perpendicular) to both these vectors in $R^3$ (three-dimensional space).  
{{<indentline>}} Using the __cross product__, we calculated vector $\mathbf{c}$. We then used the __dot product__ to _verify_ that  it was indeed orthogonal to vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.

#### 1b)
> For which values, of the parameter $a$, does the vectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = (a + 1,3))$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (2,-6)$
represent a __basis__ in $R^2$? 

### Concept: Linear Independence
Two or more vectors are said to be __linearly independent__ if we cannot create
one of the vectors by combining the others. We can think of it this way, if a vector is seen as a path to a coordinate, 
then we cannot reach that coordinate by combining the paths
of the other vectors that are linearly independet to that vector.



### Concept: Vector Space
A set (collection) of vectors. We can add them together, and also multiply each vector
by a scalar. For the set $A$ to be a __vector space__, the operations _vector addition_ and _scalar multiplication_, 
must satisfy _ten axioms_. If these axioms are satisfied, then the "vectors" $\in A$
don't have to be _arrows in 2D or 3D space_, but can be much more abstract objects, such as
_matrices, polynomials, functions, or other mathematical objects.

Axioms: closure under addition and scalar multiplication, associativity, existance of a zero vector, every vector must have an additive inverse in the set, 
$$
\text{if } u,v \in V \rightarrow u+v \in V \\\
\text{if } u \in V \text{ and } c \in R \rightarrow cv \in V\\\
$$

__Example:__  
$R^2$ is a _vector space_, that contains all the numbers, and they can be visualized as vectors on a 2D Cartesian plane.
$\langle x,y \rangle$ has the additive inverse of $\langle -x,-y \rangle$, and zero vector $\langle 0,0 \rangle$.

### Concept: Span
The __span__ of a set of vectors is the set of all possible linear combinatiosn
of those vectors. This gives us all the vectors that can be "reached" by scaling
and adding the vectors in the set.  
{{<indentline>}} For a single vector, the span are all the points along that vector
which we can reach by scaling the vector. For two non-parallel vectors, the span are all the points that are 
covered by the 2D-plane the vectors create. Any point on that plane can be reached by 
scaling xor adding the two vectors. For three non-coplanar vectors, the span is the entire 3D space.  
{{<indentline>}}  A span is always a __subspace__ of a vector space. Any vector in the vector space can be written as a unique combination (a linear combination) of the basis vectors: $\text{Span } \\{v_1, v_2 \\} = \\{ c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 \\, | \\, c_1, c_2 \in R\\}$

### Concept: Basis
A __basis__ is a set of vectors that can be used as building blocks for a __vector space__.
We can think of it as the _smallest possible set of vectors_ need to _build_ the entire vector space.
For the _set of vectors_ to be used as a basis, it must satify two conditions:
1. All the vectors that are members of the set must be __linearly independent__.
2. The vectors must __span__ the space. 

__Example__:  
A common example of a basis is the __standard basis__ of the _2D Cartesioan plane_, $R^2$.
This plane has two axes, $\langle x, y \rangle$, and so our basis consists of the 
two vectors $i=\langle 1,0 \rangle$ and $j=\langle 0,1 \rangle$.
We can check conditions for these to vectors $i$ and $j$ to be a basis:
1. Linearly independet? Yes. We cannot multiply $i$ by any scalar to get $j$, and vice versa.
2. Span? Yes. We can create any vector $w \in R^2$ by scalar multiplication of the basis. For exmaple $w = \langle 4,7 \rangle$ can be created by $w = 4i - 7j = 4 \langle 1,0 \rangle - 7 \langle 0,1 \rangle = \langle 4,-7 \rangle$.

We can check if a two vectors $\in R^2$ are linearly independent by calculating their
__determinant__. __If the determinant isn't $0$, then we have a basis.__

__Solution:__  
So the technique is to form a matrix containing our two vectors, $\mathbf{v}_1 = (a + 1,3)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (2,-6)$:
by creating two colons of the vectors, and placing them into a matrix:

$$
\begin{align}
A &=
\begin{vmatrix}
    a + 1 & 3 \\\
    2 & -6 \\\
    \end{vmatrix} \\\
\end{align}
$$

Remember, the two vectors $v_1$ and $v_2$ form a basis, _unless_ $det(A) = 0$.
So we need to calculate the value of $a$ where $det(A) = 0$. We start with the determinant of $A$.

$$
\begin{align}
det(A)  &= (a + 1)(-6) - (3)(2) \\\
&= (-6a + -6) - 6 \\\
&= -6a - 12 \\\
\end{align}
$$

Next we check the value of $a$ for which $det(A) = 0$:
$$
-6a-12 = 0 \\\
a = \frac{12}{-6} \\\
a = -2
$$


### Solution
The value $a=-2$ is the only one where the vectors $v_1$ and $v_2$ do _not_ form a basis.

### Concept: Parameterized Form

$$\vec{r}(t) = \vec{r}_0 + t \vec{v}$$
where $\vec{r}_0$ is a point on the line and $t \vec{v}$ is its _direction vector_.

We can find the intersection of two lines by rewriting them in _parameterized_ form
and solve for equality (s = t):

$$\text{2.) }(x,y): x= 2+s, y=3-s$$
